# National and Ontario brands for CCCL (researched October 2026)

Research only: nobody has been contacted. Contacts are the brand's own official pages, emails or phone numbers. 'Confidence' is how solid the facts and route are, not the odds of a yes. Re-check a brand's page before Cody pitches it.

## How to pitch a brand (different from local businesses)
- **Ask for product first, cash later.** Year 1: product for fighter bags, a sampling table, prize packs or gear, plus logo placement. Ask for cash once there are real 2027 crowd and livestream numbers.
- **Use their own route.** Most brands have a sponsorship form or a partnership page. Fill it in with Cody's details; don't cold-email random staff.
- **Lead with the facts in cccl-facts.md**: OAC-sanctioned amateur MMA, 400 tickets at the first show, 15 sponsors, 4+ shows in 2027 across Ontario. No made-up reach or follower numbers.
- **In-kind sponsors go on the sheet as 'In-kind Partner'.** Only cash counts toward the sponsorship goal.
- **The $300 prize cap.** Anything a fighter receives (prizes, gift cards, gear) must be worth under $300 in total, or they lose amateur status. Belts, medals and trophies are fine. No raffle without a lottery licence.

## Who can sit next to the kids
Youth grappling is on the card, so:
- **Youth-safe**: BioSteel, Canadian Protein, Tim Hortons, Home Hardware, Hayabusa, STING, Kubota, Ford.
- **Keep away from youth bouts and kids' content**: energy drinks (Monster, GURU; the Canadian Beverage Association restricts energy-drink marketing where 30%+ of the audience is under 12), alcohol, gambling and betting.
- **Never**: cannabis or vape sponsors.

## Alcohol rules (a summary, not legal advice)

This summarizes the rules; it isn't legal advice. Nothing found bans an alcohol brand from sponsoring a combat event in Ontario outright, but several overlapping rules shape what it can look like. (1) AGCO Registrar's advertising guidelines for liquor sales licensees and manufacturers. They cover all advertising that carries the brand's name, including authorized ads placed by others such as a promoter. Ads must not appeal directly or indirectly to people under 19 (Guideline 4). No well-known personality who may appeal to minors (4.1). No medium targeted at minors (4.4). No liquor-endorsing ads in areas specifically targeted at minors (4.5). Ads must not imply drinking brings athletic prowess (3). Liquor can't be associated with activities requiring care and skill or with elements of danger: anyone shown with liquor must clearly be a spectator or finished for the day (5, 5.3). A contest a manufacturer sponsors but someone else runs doesn't have to be adults-only, but a manufacturer's own contest does (4.7). (2) AGCO manufacturer-inducement rules: a manufacturer may not directly or indirectly give a licensee financial or material inducements to increase sales of its brand, or pay for a licensee's advertising. This matters if the venue or the promoter's Special Occasion Permit bar pours the sponsor's product. (3) Special Occasion Permits: public-event permit holders may advertise liquor availability only under the Registrar's standards, and liquor prizes can only go to people 19+. (4) The CRTC Code for Broadcast Advertising of Alcoholic Beverages covers broadcast commercials. No ads directed at minors. No association with activities attractive mainly to minors; the interpretation warns against events whose audience or participants are mainly underage. No endorsements by role models for minors. No linking to activities needing skill or alertness. (5) The Spirits Canada Code of Responsible Advertising and Marketing is voluntary for Spirits Canada members but covers all media, including digital and sponsorships. Advertising goes only where at least 70% of the audience is reasonably expected to be of legal age, and it shouldn't target events unless 70%+ of the audience is legal age (II.8, II.9). Digital marketing needs age affirmation (IX.1). Models must be 25+. The US DISCUS code (relevant to US-headquartered suppliers) uses a 73.8% adult-audience threshold. (6) Ontario Athletics Commission: the public OAC amateur combative sports pages and MMA rules pages we read have no rule on alcohol sponsorship or advertising. The full text of the Combative Sports Act regulations (O. Reg. 507/24 and 565/24) couldn't be machine-read, so this isn't confirmed. (7) Municipal Alcohol Policies at arenas can be stricter. Example: Zorra Township's MAP (s.14.2) bans alcohol advertising at youth-frequented facilities, and Sarnia's MAP discourages mixed-age licensed events. A.J. LaRue Arena belongs to the Municipality of Dysart et al, so its MAP applies; we did not find that policy online.

**The youth problem**: The youth grappling matches are the main blocker. With kids competing and families watching, an alcohol brand would struggle to show the event meets the 70%-legal-age audience test (Spirits Canada II.9). It would also risk the AGCO rules against appealing indirectly to minors and against placing liquor ads in areas aimed at minors, and the CRTC interpretation about events with mainly underage participants or audiences. Practical fixes to ask the AGCO or a lawyer about: run youth grappling at a separate session or date with no alcohol branding; keep alcohol logos off youth bouts, youth graphics, the cage during youth matches and any content featuring kids; restrict the alcohol activation to a 19+ VIP or bar area or after-party; never use fighters (role models) holding or endorsing the product before or during competition; and get a written read from the venue owner on its Municipal Alcohol Policy.

**Posts and the livestream**: AGCO guidelines apply to any advertising carrying the brand, whatever the medium, so posts, the livestream and highlight clips all count. The CRTC Code is written for broadcast; a Lexpert summary notes it may not formally cover social media, but it's treated as the industry standard. Spirits Canada members must put digital marketing only where 70%+ of the audience is legal age, require age affirmation (age-gating) on brand-controlled digital content, label it as brand marketing, and moderate user comments. In practice: don't put alcohol logos on any livestream or channel whose audience may skew under 19 (check the audience-age data of every channel used, including any gaming or YouTube channels that cross-promote the show). Use a separate 19+ audience-restricted post or segment for the alcohol sponsor, and keep it out of youth highlight clips. Ontario's iGaming rules are separate but similar: since Feb 28, 2024, the AGCO bars registered igaming operators from using active or retired athletes in ads (except for responsible-gambling messages) and restricts any figures likely to appeal to minors. A betting sponsor on a card with youth matches and fighter-heavy promo is very high-risk.

**Before signing any alcohol or betting sponsor, confirm with**: Before signing any alcohol (or betting) sponsor: (a) AGCO, on advertising and the manufacturer-inducement question if the event bar pours the sponsor's product, and which Special Occasion Permit or licence applies; (b) the Ontario Athletics Commission, on any rules about sponsor logos on attire, canvas or cage, and alcohol at sanctioned amateur events, especially cards with youth; (c) each venue owner or municipality (Dysart et al for A.J. LaRue Arena; Peterborough, Kawartha Lakes, Kingston, Oshawa venues), on its Municipal Alcohol Policy and any ban on alcohol advertising at youth-frequented facilities; (d) an Ontario advertising/liquor lawyer to review the sponsorship contract and ad creative. Ad Standards offers an alcohol advertising clearance service for creative.
